A BTU calculator tells you how much cooling power a room air conditioner needs. Window, through-the-wall and portable units are all rated in **British thermal units per hour (BTU/h)**: the amount of heat they can pull out of a room every hour. Buy too little and the room never gets comfortable on a hot afternoon. Buy too much and the unit short-cycles, wastes electricity and leaves the air damp.

This calculator uses the sizing method published by ENERGY STAR, the US Environmental Protection Agency’s efficiency programme. It is designed for a single room. If you are sizing central air or a heat pump for a whole house, use the <CalcLink slug="ac-tonnage-calculator">AC tonnage calculator</CalcLink> as a first estimate and ask an installer for a Manual J load calculation.

## How the BTU calculation works

ENERGY STAR starts from the room’s floor area and a sizing chart that assumes 8 ft (2.4 m) ceilings. It then adjusts for the three things that most change how much heat a room gains.

<Formula>
Capacity (BTU/h) = Chart value × Sun factor + 600 × (People − 2) + Kitchen allowance
</Formula>

- **Chart value**: the capacity for your floor area from the table below.
- **Sun factor**: 0.9 for a heavily shaded room, 1.0 for average, 1.1 for a very sunny room.
- **People**: each person gives off heat, so add 600 BTU/h for every regular occupant beyond two.
- **Kitchen allowance**: 4,000 BTU/h if the room is a kitchen, to cover heat from cooking.

| Floor area (sq ft) | Floor area (m²) | Capacity (BTU/h) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 to 150 | 9.3 to 13.9 | 5,000 |
| 150 to 250 | 13.9 to 23.2 | 6,000 |
| 250 to 300 | 23.2 to 27.9 | 7,000 |
| 300 to 350 | 27.9 to 32.5 | 8,000 |
| 350 to 400 | 32.5 to 37.2 | 9,000 |
| 400 to 450 | 37.2 to 41.8 | 10,000 |
| 450 to 550 | 41.8 to 51.1 | 12,000 |
| 550 to 700 | 51.1 to 65.0 | 14,000 |
| 700 to 1,000 | 65.0 to 92.9 | 18,000 |
| 1,000 to 1,200 | 92.9 to 111.5 | 21,000 |
| 1,200 to 1,400 | 111.5 to 130.1 | 23,000 |
| 1,400 to 1,500 | 130.1 to 139.4 | 24,000 |
| 1,500 to 2,000 | 139.4 to 185.8 | 30,000 |
| 2,000 to 2,500 | 185.8 to 232.3 | 34,000 |

Each band includes its lower number and runs up to, but not including, the next. The square-metre column is our conversion; ENERGY STAR publishes square feet only.

## Worked example

Say you want to cool an open-plan kitchen and dining room measuring 20 ft by 20 ft (400 sq ft). It faces west and gets the afternoon sun, and three people usually eat there.

1. 400 sq ft is in the 400 to 450 band, so the chart value is 10,000 BTU/h.
2. Very sunny: 10,000 × 1.1 = 11,000 BTU/h.
3. One person over two: + 600 BTU/h.
4. It is a kitchen: + 4,000 BTU/h.

The room needs about **15,600 BTU/h**, roughly 4.57 kW or 1.3 tons. Without the kitchen and the extra person it would need only 11,000 BTU/h, which shows how much those adjustments matter.

## How to read your result

Room air conditioners are sold in steps, so you will rarely find a unit that exactly matches your number. Pick the closest size **at or slightly above** the result. Going one step up is fine; going two or three steps up is where clammy, short-cycling problems start.

Outside the US you will often see capacity in kilowatts, which is why the result is also shown in kW. Split systems in Australia, the UK and the Gulf are commonly labelled in kW (for example 2.5 kW, 3.5 kW, 5 kW), while many are also marketed as “9,000 BTU” or “12,000 BTU”. In the US, central systems are sold in tons, where one ton equals 12,000 BTU/h. For a quick conversion between the two, use the <CalcLink slug="btu-to-watts-converter">BTU to watts converter</CalcLink>.

As a rough sense check, most bedrooms land between 5,000 and 8,000 BTU/h and most living rooms between 8,000 and 14,000 BTU/h. If your result is far outside that, double-check the area you entered.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the whole floor plan.** Enter only the space the unit will actually cool. A window unit in a bedroom will not cool the hallway, even if the door is open.
- **Ignoring the sun.** A sunny room with large west-facing windows can need noticeably more capacity than the same room on the shady side of the house.
- **Forgetting the kitchen.** Cooking adds a lot of heat. ENERGY STAR’s 4,000 BTU/h kitchen allowance is larger than the gap between several chart bands.
- **Assuming bigger is better.** An oversized unit removes heat fast but not moisture, so the room feels cold and damp. It also uses more power starting and stopping.
- **High ceilings.** The chart assumes 8 ft ceilings. Vaulted or double-height rooms hold far more air, so the chart will undersize them.
- **Using a room chart for a whole house.** Whole-house systems depend on insulation, windows, duct leaks and your climate. That needs a proper load calculation.
