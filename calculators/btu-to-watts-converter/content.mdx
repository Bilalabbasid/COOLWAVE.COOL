This converter turns BTU per hour into watts, kilowatts and tons of cooling, and back again. It also converts BTU as an amount of energy into watt-hours and kilowatt-hours. Use it to compare an American air conditioner rated in BTU with a European or Australian one rated in kW, to check a generator or solar setup, or just to make sense of a spec sheet.

Most people who search “BTU to watts” are looking at an air conditioner, heat pump or heater. Those labels say “12,000 BTU”, but the number is really **BTU per hour**, a rate. That is why the converter starts on BTU/h.

## BTU vs BTU/h: the difference that matters

A **British thermal unit (BTU)** is an amount of energy: roughly the heat needed to warm one pound of water by 1 °F, according to the EIA. It sits in the same family as the joule, the watt-hour and the kilowatt-hour.

**BTU per hour (BTU/h)** is how fast that energy moves. It sits in the same family as the watt and the kilowatt. A 12,000 BTU/h air conditioner can remove 12,000 BTU of heat from a room every hour it runs flat out.

| You have | It measures | Convert it to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BTU/h (AC, heater, furnace rating) | Power, a rate | W, kW or tons |
| BTU (gas use, fuel content) | Energy, an amount | Wh or kWh |
| W or kW | Power, a rate | BTU/h |
| kWh (electric bill) | Energy, an amount | BTU |

Mixing them up is the most common mistake on this topic. “12,000 BTU = 3.5 kWh” is wrong; 12,000 BTU/h is 3.5 kW, and it becomes 3.5 kWh only after running for one hour.

## The conversion formulas

The factors come from NIST Special Publication 811, the US government guide to SI units. The BTU used on HVAC equipment is the International Table BTU.

<Formula>
Watts = BTU/h × 0.29307107
</Formula>

<Formula>
BTU/h = Watts × 3.41214
</Formula>

<Formula>
Tons = BTU/h ÷ 12,000 (1 ton = 3,516.85 W)
</Formula>

<Formula>
kWh = BTU × 0.00029307107 (1 kWh = 3,412.14 BTU)
</Formula>

- **0.29307107** is NIST’s figure for one BTU per hour in watts.
- **3.41214** is simply 1 ÷ 0.29307107.
- **12,000 BTU/h** is one ton of refrigeration, which NIST lists as 3,516.853 W.
- Because one BTU per hour sustained for one hour is one BTU, the energy factor (BTU to Wh) is the same 0.29307107.

## Cooling watts are not electrical watts

The watts this converter gives for an air conditioner are **heat watts**: how much heat the unit moves. A heat pump or AC moves more heat than the electricity it uses, so its electrical draw is much lower. To estimate the power it pulls from the wall, divide the BTU/h rating by its EER (BTU/h of cooling per watt of electricity), which ENERGY STAR lists for certified models. A 12,000 BTU/h unit with an EER of 12 draws about 1,000 W.

Electric resistance heaters are different: almost every watt becomes heat, so a 1,500 W heater really does put out about 5,118 BTU/h. Once you know the electrical watts, the <CalcLink slug="watts-to-kwh-calculator">watts to kWh calculator</CalcLink> turns them into energy use and cost.

## Common mistakes

- **Converting an AC rating to kWh.** The label is BTU per hour, so the answer is in watts or kW. kWh only appears once you multiply by hours of running.
- **Treating cooling watts as power draw.** A 3.5 kW cooling unit does not need a 3.5 kW generator or circuit; check the electrical input on the nameplate.
- **Using 3.4 or 0.3 as the factor.** Fine for a quick guess, but 0.3 overstates watts by about 2%. Use 0.29307107 when the numbers matter.
- **Confusing tons of cooling with weight.** One ton of AC is a rate of 12,000 BTU/h, not a mass.
