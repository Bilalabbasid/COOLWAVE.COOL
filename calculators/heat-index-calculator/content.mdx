The heat index combines air temperature and relative humidity into one number: an estimate of how hot the conditions feel to a person in the shade. It was developed by R. G. Steadman in 1979 and is the measure the US National Weather Service (NWS) uses to issue heat advisories and warnings. This calculator uses the exact NWS algorithm, works in Fahrenheit or Celsius, and tells you which NWS warning level your result falls in.

It is useful for anyone planning work, sport or travel in hot weather, for parents and carers deciding whether it is safe to be outdoors, and for students checking a weather-science assignment.

## The heat index formula

The NWS calculates the heat index in two stages. First it tries a simple formula that agrees with Steadman’s results at lower values:

<Formula>
HI = 0.5 × (T + 61.0 + (T − 68.0) × 1.2 + RH × 0.094)
</Formula>

If the average of that result and the temperature is 80 °F or higher, it uses the more accurate **Rothfusz regression** instead:

<Formula>
HI = −42.379 + 2.04901523·T + 10.14333127·RH − 0.22475541·T·RH − 0.00683783·T² − 0.05481717·RH² + 0.00122874·T²·RH + 0.00085282·T·RH² − 0.00000199·T²·RH²
</Formula>

- **T** is the air temperature in °F.
- **RH** is the relative humidity in percent, for example 50 for 50%.

Two corrections follow. In very dry air (RH below 13% at 80 to 112 °F), a small amount is subtracted. In very humid air (RH above 85% at 80 to 87 °F), a small amount is added. If you enter °C, we convert to °F, run the same steps, and convert the result back.

## What your result means

The NWS groups heat index values into four levels:

| Heat index | NWS level | Possible effects with prolonged exposure or activity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 80–90 °F (27–32 °C) | Caution | Fatigue possible |
| 90–103 °F (32–39 °C) | Extreme caution | Heat stroke, heat cramps or heat exhaustion possible |
| 103–124 °F (39–51 °C) | Danger | Heat cramps or heat exhaustion likely; heat stroke possible |
| 125 °F (52 °C) or more | Extreme danger | Heat stroke highly likely |

Local thresholds for heat advisories vary. Places that are used to heat often wait for higher values before issuing warnings, so always follow your local forecast office or national weather service.

## Accuracy and limits

The Rothfusz regression is a curve fitted to Steadman’s tables. The NWS says it is accurate to about **±1.3 °F**. We test this calculator against more than 50 cells of the official NWS chart. Almost all agree within 1.5 °F; the exception is the most humid corner of the chart (80 °F at 95 to 100% humidity), where the official high-humidity correction reads about 2 °F above the printed chart. We follow the published formula exactly rather than adjust it.

The formula is not valid for extreme combinations of heat and humidity beyond the range of Steadman’s data. That is why this calculator gives a warning, not a number, above 110 °F or when the heat index would exceed about 137 °F. It is also not meant for cool weather: below 80 °F the heat index stays close to the air temperature, and in the cold you should use the <CalcLink slug="wind-chill-calculator">wind chill calculator</CalcLink>.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a sun-baked thermometer.** Car and porch thermometers in direct sun read high. Use shade temperatures, as weather stations do.
- **Entering the dew point as humidity.** Relative humidity is a percentage. If you only know the dew point, convert it first with the <CalcLink slug="relative-humidity-calculator">relative humidity calculator</CalcLink>.
- **Treating the number as a safety guarantee.** Heat illness can occur at lower values for children, older people, people on some medications and anyone exercising hard.
- **Forgetting the sun.** Working in direct sunlight can add up to 15 °F to the value shown here.
