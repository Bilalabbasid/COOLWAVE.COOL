This calculator answers the question most people ask before plugging in a heater for winter: how much will it add to the electricity bill? Choose the common 1,500 W or 750 W settings, or enter your own heater’s wattage, add how long you run it and your price per kWh, and you get the cost per hour, per day and per month. An extra input lets you account for the thermostat switching the heater off once the room is warm, which is the main reason real bills come in lower than the label suggests.

## The space heater cost formula

Electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh is 1,000 watts running for one hour, so a heater’s wattage tells you directly how fast it uses energy.

<Formula>
Average power (W) = Heater wattage × Share of time heating
Cost per hour = Average power ÷ 1,000 × Price per kWh
Cost per day = Cost per hour × Hours used per day
Cost per month = Cost per day × Days used per month
</Formula>

- **Heater wattage** is on the label, usually on the back or base. Most household plug-in heaters top out at 1,500 W: when CPSC staff tested 27 household portable electric heaters in 2023, 23 of them had a maximum of 1,500 W.
- **Share of time heating** is 100% if the heater never switches off. With a thermostat, it cycles on and off to hold the temperature, so the true figure depends on how cold the room is and how well it holds heat.
- **Price per kWh** is the unit rate on your bill as a decimal, for example 0.18 for 18 cents or 0.27 for 27p.

## Worked example

You run a 1,500 W heater on high for 8 hours a day through a 30-day month, and you pay $0.18 per kWh.

On full power the heater uses 1.5 kWh an hour, which costs **$0.27 an hour**, $2.16 for the 8 hours and **$64.80 for the month**, from 360 kWh of electricity. If its thermostat keeps the heating element on only half the time once the room is warm, the average draw falls to 750 W and the month costs $32.40 instead. The same is true of switching to a 750 W low setting.

## What the result means

To put the numbers in context, the EIA puts the average US household’s electricity use at about 10,500 kWh a year, roughly 875 kWh a month. A single heater running long hours can add a large share of that on its own:

| Use pattern (1,500 W, full power) | kWh per month | Cost at $0.18/kWh |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 hours a day, 30 days | 90 | $16.20 |
| 4 hours a day, 30 days | 180 | $32.40 |
| 8 hours a day, 30 days | 360 | $64.80 |
| 12 hours a day, 30 days | 540 | $97.20 |

A space heater saves money only when it lets you heat one room instead of the whole house. If the central heating carries on as normal, the heater is pure extra cost. ## Common mistakes

- **Treating the label as the real use.** The label is the maximum. A heater with a working thermostat in a well-sealed room might heat only part of each hour. A plug-in energy monitor shows the real kWh after a few days.
- **Mixing up watts and kilowatts.** A heater rated 1.5 kW is 1,500 W. Typing 1.5 into the watts box makes the answer a thousand times too small.
- **Using the whole bill divided by kWh.** Fixed daily or monthly charges are not part of the price per kWh. Use the unit rate printed on the bill.
- **Assuming a “high efficiency” heater uses less per hour.** Two 1,500 W heaters on full power cost the same. Controls and thermostats make the difference, not the heating element type.
- **Running it while you sleep.** It adds hours and cost, and CPSC advises against it on safety grounds.

## Safety comes first

Space heaters account for about a third of US home heating equipment fires but nearly nine out of ten of the deaths, according to NFPA figures for 2016–2020. The Consumer Product Safety Commission’s advice is simple: keep every side of the heater at least 3 feet from beds, curtains, sofas and anything else that can burn, plug it straight into a wall outlet rather than a power strip, set it on a stable, level surface, and never leave it running while you sleep.
