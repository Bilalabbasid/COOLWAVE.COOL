The heat index is how hot it actually feels to your body when humidity is combined with the air temperature. The National Weather Service (NWS) defines it as a measure of "how hot it really feels" once relative humidity is factored in, so 96 °F with 65% humidity feels like 121 °F. It is the number behind summer heat advisories, and it is calculated for shade, not sun.

The rest of this guide explains where the number comes from, how to read a heat index chart, and what each danger level means.

## Why humidity makes hot air feel hotter

Your body sheds heat mainly by sweating. Sweat only cools you when it evaporates, and evaporation slows down when the air already holds a lot of water vapor. The NWS Amarillo office puts it simply: when relative humidity is high, the rate of evaporation from the body drops, so the body feels warmer than the air temperature suggests. In dry air the opposite happens and you can feel cooler than the thermometer reading.

That is why 90 °F in Phoenix and 90 °F in Houston are not the same day. At 90 °F and 20% humidity the heat index is about 86 °F. At 90 °F and 70% humidity it is about 106 °F, a 20-degree difference from the same thermometer reading.

## How the heat index is calculated

The heat index comes from work by Robert Steadman, who modeled how a clothed adult exchanges heat with the air. In 1990 Lans Rothfusz of the NWS fitted a regression equation to Steadman's tables, and that equation, with two small adjustments, is what the NWS Weather Prediction Center publishes today:

<Formula>HI = -42.379 + 2.04901523 T + 10.14333127 RH - 0.22475541 T RH - 0.00683783 T² - 0.05481717 RH² + 0.00122874 T² RH + 0.00085282 T RH² - 0.00000199 T² RH²</Formula>

Here T is the air temperature in °F and RH is relative humidity in percent. A few details matter:

- For milder conditions (a result below about 80 °F), the NWS uses a simpler formula instead, because the regression is not meant for that range.
- Two corrections are applied at the edges: a downward adjustment when humidity is below 13% between 80 and 112 °F, and an upward one when humidity is above 85% between 80 and 87 °F.
- The NWS says the regression has an error of about ±1.3 °F, so treat the result as a close estimate, not a laboratory reading.

The heat index is only defined for shade and a light breeze. The NWS warns that full sunshine can add up to 15 °F, and strong winds with very hot, dry air can also be hazardous.

## What the heat index danger levels mean

The NWS groups heat index values into four classifications. These describe the risk with prolonged exposure or physical activity, not a guarantee of illness.

| Heat index | NWS classification | Possible effect on the body |
|---|---|---|
| 80–90 °F | Caution | Fatigue possible with prolonged exposure and/or physical activity |
| 90–103 °F | Extreme caution | Heat stroke, heat cramps or heat exhaustion possible |
| 103–124 °F | Danger | Heat cramps or heat exhaustion likely; heat stroke possible |
| 125 °F and above | Extreme danger | Heat stroke highly likely |

Local NWS offices issue heat advisories and warnings based on forecast heat index values, but the exact thresholds differ by region because people in hot climates are more used to heat. Always follow the alerts for your area.

## Heat index vs. other heat measures

The heat index is not the only number you will see in summer forecasts:

- **Dew point** tells you how much moisture is in the air, regardless of temperature. Many forecasters prefer it for judging mugginess. Our dew point calculator{/* TODO-LINK:dew-point-calculator */} converts temperature and humidity into dew point.
- **Wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT)** is used for athletes and outdoor workers. The NWS describes it as an estimate of heat stress in direct sunlight that combines temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation, while the heat index describes how the temperature feels in shade.
- **Wet-bulb temperature** is the lowest temperature evaporation can cool something to. You can estimate it with our wet bulb calculator{/* TODO-LINK:wet-bulb-calculator */}.

## Staying safe when the heat index is high

This section is general information, not medical advice. Heat illness can happen below the thresholds above, especially for young children, older adults, people with chronic conditions and anyone working or exercising outdoors.

The CDC's National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) lists headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness, strong thirst, heavy sweating and reduced urination as symptoms of heat exhaustion. It lists confusion, slurred speech, seizures, very high body temperature and loss of consciousness as signs of heat stroke, which can be fatal without prompt treatment. For suspected heat stroke, NIOSH says to call 911, move the person to a cool, shaded area and cool them quickly.

Practical steps when the heat index climbs:

1. Check the heat index, not just the temperature, before outdoor work or exercise.
2. Schedule strenuous activity for early morning or evening.
3. Spend time in air conditioning when possible. If you are sizing a unit for a hot room, our <CalcLink slug="btu-calculator">BTU calculator</CalcLink> can help.
4. Drink water regularly and take breaks in the shade.
5. Check on neighbors and relatives who live alone or lack cooling.

## The bottom line

The heat index answers a simple question: how hot does it feel to a person in the shade? It combines temperature and humidity with an NWS regression that is accurate to about ±1.3 °F, and values of 103 °F or more fall in the NWS Danger range.
