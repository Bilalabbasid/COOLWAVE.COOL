The dew point is the temperature the air would have to cool to, with no change in pressure or moisture, before it becomes saturated at 100% relative humidity. Cool it any further and water vapor condenses into dew, fog or cloud. The higher the dew point, the more moisture is in the air, which is why forecasters use it as the best single number for how muggy it will feel: dew points in the 50s °F feel comfortable to most people, and the 70s feel oppressive.

## Dew point vs. relative humidity

Relative humidity (RH) and dew point both describe moisture, but they answer different questions.

- **Relative humidity** is a percentage: how much water vapor the air holds compared with the most it could hold at its current temperature. Because warm air can hold more vapor than cold air, the same moisture gives a lower RH on a hot afternoon and a higher RH at night.
- **Dew point** is a temperature that depends only on the amount of water vapor. It barely changes from afternoon to night unless a new air mass moves in.

The NWS office in La Crosse, Wisconsin uses a clear example. At 30 °F with a 30 °F dew point, relative humidity is 100%. At 80 °F with a 60 °F dew point, it is only about 50%. Yet the 80-degree day feels far more humid, because there is much more water in the air. For judging how sticky it will feel outside, the dew point is the more honest number.

Here is the same idea in a table. The dew point stays at 60 °F, and only the air temperature changes:

| Air temperature | Dew point | Relative humidity |
|---|---|---|
| 60 °F | 60 °F | 100% |
| 70 °F | 60 °F | 71% |
| 80 °F | 60 °F | 51% |
| 90 °F | 60 °F | 37% |
| 100 °F | 60 °F | 27% |

## Dew point comfort chart

The NWS does not publish a single national comfort scale, but its local offices describe very similar ranges. La Crosse calls 55 °F or less "dry and comfortable", 55 to 65 °F "becoming sticky with muggy evenings", and 65 °F or more "becoming oppressive". The Louisville office says dew points in the 50s are comfortable to most people, the 60s somewhat uncomfortable, and the 70s quite uncomfortable. Combining those two descriptions:

| Dew point (°F) | Dew point (°C) | How it typically feels in summer |
|---|---|---|
| Below 50 | Below 10 | Dry, crisp; skin and lips may feel dry |
| 50–55 | 10–13 | Comfortable for most people |
| 55–60 | 13–16 | Pleasant to slightly humid |
| 60–65 | 16–18 | Sticky, muggy evenings |
| 65–70 | 18–21 | Humid and uncomfortable for many people |
| 70 and above | 21 and above | Oppressive; sweat evaporates poorly |

Comfort is personal. People who live with humid summers often tolerate higher dew points than visitors from dry climates.

## How to calculate the dew point

The NWS weather calculator uses a Magnus-type formula for vapor pressure. With temperatures in °C:

<Formula>es = 6.11 × 10^(7.5 T / (237.3 + T))</Formula>

Here es is the saturation vapor pressure in hectopascals (hPa) at air temperature T. The actual vapor pressure e is es multiplied by RH/100. To find the dew point, run the formula backwards:

<Formula>Td = 237.3 x / (7.5 − x), where x = log10(e / 6.11)</Formula>

### Worked example: 80 °F at 50% relative humidity

1. Convert to Celsius: 80 °F = 26.67 °C.
2. Saturation vapor pressure: es = 6.11 × 10^(7.5 × 26.67 / 263.97) = 6.11 × 10^0.7577 ≈ 34.97 hPa.
3. Actual vapor pressure: e = 34.97 × 0.50 ≈ 17.49 hPa.
4. x = log10(17.49 / 6.11) = log10(2.862) ≈ 0.4566.
5. Td = 237.3 × 0.4566 / (7.5 − 0.4566) ≈ 108.36 / 7.043 ≈ 15.4 °C.
6. Convert back: 15.4 °C ≈ 59.7 °F.

So a summer afternoon of 80 °F and 50% humidity has a dew point of about 60 °F: right at the line where it starts to feel sticky. The same formula is behind our <CalcLink slug="relative-humidity-calculator">relative humidity calculator</CalcLink>, which works in the other direction, from temperature and dew point to RH.

A quick rule of thumb you may see is "subtract one fifth of (100 − RH) from the temperature in °C". It is only reasonable when humidity is above about 50%; use the formula or the calculator for anything that matters.

## Dew point indoors: condensation and mold

The dew point explains why windows fog, cold-water pipes drip and basements smell damp. Any surface colder than the air's dew point cools the air touching it to saturation, and water condenses on that surface.

Take a home at 70 °F and 50% relative humidity. Using the same formula, the indoor dew point is about 50.5 °F. On a cold night, the inner pane of a single-glazed window can easily drop below 50 °F, so it fogs. Lower the indoor humidity to 35% and the dew point falls to about 41 °F, and the same window stays clear.

The U.S. EPA recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60%, ideally between 30% and 50%, and notes that condensation on windows, walls or pipes can be a sign of high humidity. ## Dew point, fog and frost

Forecasters watch the gap between the air temperature and the dew point, called the dew point depression. When the evening temperature is forecast to fall to the dew point, fog or dew is likely by morning. When the dew point is below 32 °F, moisture deposits as frost instead.

## Dew point and heat safety

This is general information, not medical advice. A high dew point on its own is not dangerous, but combined with high temperatures it makes it harder for sweat to evaporate, which is what the heat index measures. ## The bottom line

The dew point tells you how much moisture is really in the air. Below 55 °F feels comfortable, 55 to 65 °F gets sticky, and 65 °F and up feels oppressive, according to NWS guidance. Unlike relative humidity, it does not swing with the time of day, which makes it the better number for planning.
